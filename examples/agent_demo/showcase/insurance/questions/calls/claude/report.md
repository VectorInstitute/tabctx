# Renewal call list: the 50 employers to call first

*Group retention team, prepared 22 November 2026. Covers plans renewing 22 Nov 2026 – 15 Feb 2027.*

## The short answer

- **Call the 50 accounts in the table below, in that order.** Their combined annual premium is **$18.6M**. The model expects about **15 of the 50** to leave if nothing changes, which would cost about **$4.7M** of premium. That is 26% of the premium at risk across all 1,200 due plans, concentrated in 4% of them.
- **Four topics cover nearly every call:**
  1. **No account review in the past year** (32 of 50 accounts: 27 recorded as zero reviews, 5 with the field blank). This is the strongest pattern in the data.
  2. **Billing friction**, meaning late payments or paying by cheque (32 of 50).
  3. **A large renewal increase driven by high claims** (30 of 50; the median increase on the list is 9.8%).
  4. **Unresolved service issues** (25 of 50).
- The model's scores are well calibrated, but it is a moderate predictor, not a crystal ball. On last year's renewals held out for testing, about 4 in 10 of its highest-risk plans actually left, against 1 in 8 overall.
- **Important caveat:** the talking points come from *patterns the model learned* from last year's leavers. None of them is a proven cause. For example, we do not know that holding a review *prevents* an employer from leaving. We only know that employers who had no review left far more often.

## How we got here (and where we changed the plan)

**1. Cleaning the exports** (`clean.py` → `renewals_2025.csv`, `renewals_due.csv`).

The two exports used several formats for the same thing:
- dates in four formats;
- province, industry, plan type, funding, channel, billing and yes/no fields each spelled 3–10 ways;
- premiums written as `$74,848.88`, `CAD 9,676` or plain numbers;
- loss ratios as both `85.6%` and `0.856`.

Other problems and how I handled them:
- **Duplicates and footer.** I removed 120 exact duplicate rows from the history and 12 from the due file, plus a `TOTAL` footer row. The result is one row per plan: 6,000 history and 1,200 due.
- **Placeholder values.** "Lives covered" placeholders (−1, 0, 99,999, 999,999) became blank. So did "n/a", "unknown" and "-" in account reviews and member app use.
- **Renewal status.** `Renewed` and `In force` count as stayed. `Terminated`, `TERM` and `moved carrier` count as left (`terminated` = Yes). Overall, 764 of 6,000 plans (12.7%) left.
- **Tenure.** I added `tenure_years` (years from the plan's effective date to its renewal date) and kept the dates themselves out of the model.

**2. How well does last year predict leaving?**

I tested the models on a random quarter of last year's plans that they had not seen:
- **Ranking.** ROC AUC is 0.77 (95% CI 0.73–0.81), against 0.73 for a standard gradient-boosting baseline.
- **Calibration.** Predicted and actual leave rates matched decile by decile; in the top 10% the model predicted 39% and 39% left. That is why the "chance of leaving" figures below can be read at face value.
- **Large accounts.** Calibration held for them too: 4.0% predicted vs 5.3% actual among the largest 5% of accounts, from only 75 plans.
- **Model choice.** TabICL and TabPFN scored the same; I used TabICL.

**3. Scoring.** I fitted the model on all 6,000 history plans and scored the 1,200 due plans (`renewals_due_predictions.csv`). It expects about 131 of the 1,200 to leave, which would cost $17.9M of $257.7M premium.

**4. Ranking: two changes to the plan.**
- **Rank by premium at risk with a risk floor.** I ranked by *premium at risk* (chance of leaving × annual premium). Ranking on that alone fills the list with very large accounts whose risk is *below* average; one $2.5M account is at 2%. I required at least the historical average risk (12.7%) before an account could make the list. On the held-out data this floor caught slightly more actual leavers in the top 50 (17 vs 13) with about the same lost premium (25% vs 23%). That difference is within noise, so the floor costs nothing measurable. It keeps the calls on accounts where there is a real retention conversation to have.
  - The large, lower-risk accounts left out are GB-410167 ($1.67M, 6%), GB-673163 ($0.89M, 10%), GB-844530 ($1.92M, 4%), GB-381367 ($0.73M, 10%) and GB-204191 ($1.15M, 6%). Their senior relationship owners should still be aware of them.
- **Plans already past their renewal date.** 79 plans in the "due" export had renewal dates of 15–21 November 2026, before today, so I left them off the list. Four of them would otherwise have ranked in the top 50: GB-794474 (18 Nov, 13%, $592K premium), GB-389851 (15 Nov, 47%), GB-453369 (19 Nov, 24%) and GB-977843 (20 Nov, 36%). Someone should check whether these renewed.

**5. What separates leavers from stayers.** I looked at this two ways:
- **Raw leave rates** in last year's data, with 95% intervals.
- **The model's view.** I changed one item at a time on held-out plans and on each call-list account, keeping everything else the same, to see how much the model's risk moves.

The bracketed "risk X% → Y%" figures in the call list come from the second method. They show **how much of that account's score the item accounts for in the model, not what a phone call will achieve.**

## What separates leavers from stayers, and how sure we are

All of these are associations in last year's data that the model has learned to use. None was tested by an experiment, and several could easily run the other way. For example, an account manager may stop scheduling reviews with a client who has already decided to leave.

| Pattern | Leave rate (raw, 2025) | What the model learned, other things equal | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No account review in 12 months** | 18.8% vs 8.0% with ≥1 review (difference +10.7 pts, 95% CI +8.9 to +12.5) | 16.8% with none vs 7.0% with one | Strong, consistent association. **Not proven causal.** One review appears to matter, but more than one adds nothing detectable: 8.0% vs 8.1%, difference +0.1 pts ± 1.9; the data could have detected a difference of about 2.7 pts. |
| **Renewal increase** | 9.8% (±0.9) for increases up to 5%; 13.3% (±2.1) for 5–8%; 17.0% (±6.0) for 8–10%; 26.8% (±3.2) above 10% | Flat at about 9% up to 4%, then rising steadily: 12% at 6%, 16% at 10%, 21% at 15% | Strong. **There is no sharp threshold.** Risk is roughly flat to 4–5% and climbs from there, and the data cannot pin the turning point closer than 4–8%. The 5–8% band is above the 0–5% band by +3.5 pts ± 2.3. |
| **Loss ratio** (claims ÷ premium) | 10.6% below 90% vs 21.7% at 90%+ | Small extra effect once the increase is known: 10% at 60% → 13% at 105% | Loss ratio and the renewal increase move together (correlation 0.79), because high claims drive the increase. The data cannot fully separate "the claims" from "the price rise". The model leans on the increase. |
| **Late payments / cheque billing** | 19.0% with a late payment vs 10.6% without; cheque payers 21.1% vs 11.2% for electronic payers | 10% with none → 15% with one → 20% with two late payments; cheque 16% vs PAD 10% | Strong association. Cheque payers have more late payments, but cheque billing stays higher-risk even among plans with the same number of late payments (+4.5 pts, CI +0.8 to +8.3, with no late payments). Could reflect the employer's financial strain rather than billing itself. |
| **Service issues** | 17.5% with ≥1 issue vs 10.7% with none (+6.9 pts, CI +4.9 to +8.9) | 10% → 15% → 21% for 0, 1, 2 issues | Strong association. |
| **Small groups / new clients** | ≤16 lives: 19.1% vs 10.9%; under 2.5 yrs tenure: 16.3% vs 11.9% | Tenure 1 yr: 16% → 15 yrs: 9% | Real patterns, but not something a call can change; treat them as context. |
| **ASO funding** | 7.3% vs 13.9% for insured/pooled | 8% vs 12–13% | Context only. |
| **Online admin, wellness program, member app use** | Each about 2–4 pts higher leave rate without | Online admin 13% vs 10%; wellness 12% vs 10%; app use 13% at 10% use vs 10% at 70% | Small effects (each CI excludes zero but sits just above what the data can detect, about 2.5 pts). Good add-ons to a call, not the main reason for it. |
| **Channel (broker, consultant, direct)** | Broker 13.1% vs others 12.4% (+0.7 pts, CI −1.0 to +2.4); direct vs consultant +0.3 pts (CI −2.2 to +2.8) | 11.3–11.6% for all three | **No detectable difference.** The data could have detected a gap of about 2.4 pts (broker vs others), so a smaller channel effect cannot be ruled out. |
| **Province** | 6–18% by province, but no overall difference beyond chance (p = 0.12) | Little weight in the model | No detectable difference. The smaller provinces have too few plans to rule out moderate differences. |

The CIs on the "model learned" column reflect only which plans were averaged. They leave out uncertainty in the model itself, so treat those figures as approximate.

## How to use the list

- **Order.** Work down the list by rank. Where renewal dates are close (the earliest are GB-829546, rank 13, on **27 Nov**; GB-979566, rank 27, on 30 Nov; and GB-546114, rank 35, on 3 Dec), move those calls forward.
- **Brokers.** 30 of the 50 are broker-placed and 13 are consultant-placed, so involve the broker or consultant in those calls.
- **Talking points.** Each account's points are listed in order of how much that item accounts for in its risk score. Each opens with the fact from our records, so the account manager can check it before the call.
- **"Context" notes** (new client, small group) are not fixable but explain why the risk is high.
- **Measure it.** To learn whether calls actually help, keep a record of who was called and what was discussed. A small random hold-back from the list (accounts we deliberately don't call) would be the only way to prove an effect. Without one, next year's data will show the same correlations, not causes.

Full list with all fields: `call_list.csv`. Scores for all 1,200 due plans: `renewals_due_predictions.csv`.
